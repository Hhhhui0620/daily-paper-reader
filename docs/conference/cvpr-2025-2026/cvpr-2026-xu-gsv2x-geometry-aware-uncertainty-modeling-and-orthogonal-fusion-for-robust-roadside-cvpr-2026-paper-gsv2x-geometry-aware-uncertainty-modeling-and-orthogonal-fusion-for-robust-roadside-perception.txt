# Abstract 

Reliable 3D perception from multi-view roadside sensors hinges on the robust fusion of camera and LiDAR data, a task complicated by geometric misalignments and sensor calibration errors. This paper presents GSV2X , a fusion framework that tackles these challenges through two core contributions. First, to achieve robustness against spatial uncertainty, we lift 2D image features into a unified Bird’s-Eye-View (BEV) space by representing them as 3D Gaus-sian distributions. By incorporating learnable perturba-tions guided by camera geometry, our model explicitly ac-counts for potential calibration inaccuracies. Second, to maximize the synergy between modalities, we propose a new orthogonal fusion module. This module employs con-strained attention to enforce orthogonality between camera and LiDAR features, effectively disentangling redundant in-formation and promoting the learning of complementary representations. Extensive experiments on the challenging RCooper dataset demonstrate that GSV2X sets a new state-of-the-art in multi-view roadside perception and exhibits re-markable robustness in complex, real-world scenarios.

The prevailing paradigm for these systems leverages multi-view, multi-modal fusion, integrating rich semantic information from cameras with precise geometric data from LiDAR [24, 28, 42]. By combining sensors at diverse viewpoints, RPS can mitigate occlusions and build a more complete 3D understanding of the scene [6, 19, 58], a research direction further propelled by large-scale datasets like RCooper [10]. # 2. Related Works 

## 2.1. Camera-based 3D Object Detection 

The Lift-Splat-Shoot (LSS) paradigm [31] revolutionized camera-based 3D object detection by providing an effec-tive pipeline to transform multi-view 2D features into a uni-fied BEV representation, and has since become the back-bone of many methods [12, 13, 24, 28]. LSS-based ap-proaches [21, 22, 47, 49] predict per-pixel depth distribu-tions to lift image features into 3D, but still rely on a deter-ministic projection, making them brittle to errors in depth estimation and camera calibration. This fragility is espe-cially pronounced in roadside perception, where diverse sensor heights and pitch angles challenge the generalization of learned depth predictors. To address this, prior works mainly follow two direc-tions. One line improves the robustness of the geometric projection, e.g., BEVHeight [47] regresses height instead of depth to reduce pitch sensitivity, and SGV3D [50] ex-ploits geometric priors to transfer instances across views for augmentation. The other compensates for projection er-rors after lifting to BEV: BEVSpread [43] smooths features via neighborhood-aware spreading, while CBR [7] avoids explicit projection through decoupled view transformations at the cost of geometric precision. In contrast to refining a fixed deterministic projection or correcting its BEV artifacts post-hoc, GSV2X relaxes the deterministic assumption it-self: each lifted feature is represented as a 3D Gaussian rather than a single point, explicitly modeling spatial un-certainty from depth ambiguity and calibration noise in the geometric representation and yielding a more robust BEV feature space. 

## 2.2. LiDAR-based 3D Object Detection 

Existing LiDAR-based 3D object detection methods are commonly grouped into three families: point-based [23, 32–35], voxel-based [4, 26, 41, 46], and point–voxel hy-brids [27, 30, 36, 38, 57]. Two representative lines are Vox-elNet [57] and PointPillars [20]. VoxelNet divides point clouds into 3D voxels, uses a VFE layer for intra-voxel aggregation, and a 3D backbone for spatial features, uni-fying extraction and box prediction end-to-end. It cap-tures local 3D shape and boundaries well, but stacked VFEs and 3D convs are costly in large, sparse scenes. By con-trast, PointPillars collapses height into “pillars,” encodes them into image-like BEV features, and performs detection with only 2D convs. Leveraging efficient 2D backbones, it preserves accuracy while reducing latency and boosting throughput. We therefore adopt PointPillars for an efficient, BEV-aligned point-cloud branch. 

## 2.3. Multimodal BEV-based Perception

The primary goal is to align and integrate rich camera semantics with precise sensor geom-etry in a shared BEV space. A dominant trend in recent works is the use of attention-based mechanisms to establish cross-modal correspondences. # 3. Method 

## 3.1. Overview 

Conventional fusion methods based on LSS [31] are brittle under feature misalignment, a common issue in real-world systems. Our framework GSV2X is designed to address this brittleness in multi-view roadside perception by target-ing two key challenges: spatial uncertainty and modal im-balance (Fig. 2). To handle spatial uncertainty from depth estimation and camera calibration errors, we redesign the lifting step from images to 3D. Rather than mapping each 2D pixel to a single fixed 3D point, we represent it as a 3D Gaussian distribution, treating the pixel location as a soft continuous region instead of a hard point. This proba-bilistic formulation naturally absorbs small geometric mis-alignments that are unavoidable in practice. The proposed Geometry-guided Depth Estimation (GDE) module enables this robust lifting and provides a reliable basis for plac-ing the Gaussians in 3D. To mitigate modal imbalance and conflict during fusion, we introduce an Orthogonal Fusion Module (OFM). Instead of simple feature concatenation, OFM uses a constrained fusion strategy with an orthogonal-ity regularizer between image and LiDAR feature spaces, promoting complementary rather than redundant represen-tations. The resulting fused BEV features are then fed into a detection head to produce the final 3D object predictions. 

## 3.2. BEV Representation via Gaussian Splatting 

The critical weakness of LSS-based methods [24, 31] is their reliance on a deterministic point-wise projection to transform 2D features into the 3D space. This process is inherently brittle, as any error in depth estimation or cam-era calibration leads to feature misalignment in the final BEV representation. To overcome this fragility, we pro-pose a probabilistic view transformer that represents the spatial contribution of each pixel not as a single point, but as a continuous 3D Gaussian distribution, inspired by Gaus-sianLSS [29]. Our key insight is that spatial uncertainty in this context stems from two distinct sources: the inherent ambiguity in depth prediction and the potential for extrinsic calibration noise. ## 3.4. Orthogonal Fusion Module 

Effective multimodal fusion must handle heterogeneous sensor data and issues such as temporal asynchrony and cal-ibration errors (e.g., in RCooper [10]). In many BEV fusion backbones, na¨ ıve designs such as simple concatenation or addition (e.g., BEVFusion [28]) can lead to modal dom-inance , where dense camera features contribute stronger gradients than sparse but geometrically precise LiDAR fea-21412 tures, so the network reaches a low training loss by relying heavily on the camera branch while underutilizing LiDAR cues where they are available. To mitigate this tendency, we propose an Orthogonal Fusion Module (OFM, see Fig. 2) that treats F BEV LiDAR as a geometric backbone and uses F BEV img 

as an enhancement branch, not to densify LiDAR or explain sparsity by non-orthogonality, but to structure fusion so that the camera branch does not trivially overwrite LiDAR guid-ance in LiDAR-dense regions and instead focuses on com-plementary cues such as appearance and distant context. As illustrated in Fig. 2(c), we first use an attention mech-anism to generate a spatial weight map A, which adaptively modulates the image features. The fusion is formulated as 

F BEV fused = F BEV LiDAR + A ⊙ F BEV img , (5) where ⊙ denotes the Hadamard product. In this formula-tion, the LiDAR pathway always provides a complete BEV representation, and the image branch contributes a residual term whose influence is controlled by A.The key to promoting complementarity lies in our or-thogonal regularization loss, Lorth . This loss discourages re-dundant projections of the camera features onto the LiDAR directions by penalizing their correlation at each spatial lo-cation. By minimizing the squared dot product between the feature vectors, we encourage the two modalities to occupy less correlated directions in the feature space:

Regularization behavior. Lorth is used as a mild auxiliary loss with a small weight, so the training objective is still dominated by the detection loss on F BEV fused . Since the fusion output is anchored by F BEV LiDAR , the network can always re-duce A and fall back to a LiDAR-dominant solution when camera signals are unreliable, while in LiDAR-sparse re-gions A is free to upweight the camera branch. Thus, OFM does not enforce LiDAR dominance everywhere, but gently reduces camera dominance in LiDAR-dense regions and en-courages a more complementary use of the two modalities. 

# 4. Experiments 

## 4.1. Experimental Setup 

Datasets and Metrics. Our approach is rigorously eval-uated on two challenging, large-scale roadside perception datasets. 

RCooper [10] is a real-world dataset featuring diverse data from intersection and corridor scenarios collected under various weather and lighting conditions. For all experi-ments, we strictly adhere to the official data splits. Follow-ing the benchmark’s protocol, we report Average Precision (AP) at multiple 3D IoU thresholds while treating all cate-gories as a single class, as non-vehicle classes constitute a negligible portion of the dataset. 

DAIR-V2X-I [52] is a vehicle-infrastructure cooperative dataset. We use the infrastructure-side subset. It contains 10,000 images with annotations for objects up to 200 meters away. We follow the official training and validation splits, as the test set is not public. Performance is measured us-ing the 40-point Average Precision (AP 3D |R40 ) metric, with results for the vehicle class categorized into Easy, Middle, and Hard difficulty levels, following the KITTI [9] protocol. 

## 4.2. Implementation Details 

Architecture. Our GSV2X framework is implemented in PyTorch, building upon the open-source projects Gaus-sianLSS [29] and PointPillars [20]. For the image branch, we employ a ResNet-50 [11] backbone with a Feature Pyra-mid Network (FPN) to extract multi-scale features. In-put images are resized to 512 × 512 for RCooper and 

864 × 1536 for DAIR-V2X-I to align with their respec-tive standard practices. For the LiDAR branch, we use a PointPillars backbone. The voxel size for RCooper is set to 

[0 .5, 0.5, 11] m within a point cloud range of [−100 , 100] mfor the X/Y axes and [−11 , 0] m for the Z-axis. For the BEV representation, we render features onto a 200 × 200 grid for RCooper (representing a 100 m×100 m area) and a 128 ×128 

grid for DAIR-V2X-I ( 102 .4m × 102 .4m area). 

Training and Inference. All models are trained from scratch using the AdamW optimizer [56] with a weight decay of 1 × 10 −4 and a cosine annealing learning rate schedule. For the RCooper experiments, we train for 50 epochs with a batch size of 4 and an initial learning rate of 

2 × 10 −3. For DAIR-V2X-I, we follow its common proto-col, training for 100 epochs with an initial learning rate of 

2×10 −4. Standard data augmentation techniques, including random scaling and rotation, are applied to the image data during training. All experiments are conducted on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPU with 24GB of memory.

# 5. Conclusion 

We present GSV2X, a robust framework for multi-view roadside perception that tackles two key challenges: spa-tial uncertainty and modal redundancy. Visual features are modeled as probabilistic 3D Gaussians to absorb calibra-tion and depth errors, while an Orthogonal Fusion Module encourages complementary rather than redundant contribu-tions from camera and LiDAR. Experiments on RCooper demonstrate strong performance and generalization in com-plex real-world scenarios, validating the approach, but also highlight typical limitations: GSV2X still assumes reason-ably good calibration and LiDAR availability and intro-duces extra computation, so future work will aim to relax these assumptions and reduce the overhead. 21416 Acknowledgements
